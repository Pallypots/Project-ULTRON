# Make ULTRON talk back in seconds

ULTRON is slow because of how its voice pipeline is built, not because it picked the wrong models. Every one of its hops waits for the previous one to finish completely, and the AI "brain" re-reads its whole instruction sheet and the whole conversation on every turn because the top of that sheet changes each time. The fastest local assistants of 2025–2026 (GLaDOS, Parlor, Ming Voice, RealtimeVoiceChat) use almost the same parts: Gemma 4 E2B/E4B, Kokoro, Silero VAD. What they do differently is overlap the stages, keep the prompt cache warm, detect the end of a sentence in about 200 ms, and start speaking after the first clause. Those projects report **0.23–0.7 s from end of speech to first audio**, but **all on a GPU or Apple Silicon**. No surveyed project gets under a second with a 2B+ model on CPU alone. The best CPU-only reference gets about **1.5–2 s** with a 0.5B model. For ULTRON on a CPU-only Windows PC, the realistic target is therefore **about 1.5–3 s to the first spoken word**, down from an estimated **5–8+ s before the voice even starts** (and 25–33 s per spoken reply in CI). Most of that gain comes from configuration and plumbing that can ship in days. Reaching sub-second "Jarvis" speed needs a GPU (even an enabled iGPU), and that is the most important hardware question to answer first. The roadmap below orders roughly 30 changes into four phases. Each has an expected latency effect, an effort estimate, and a test with positive and negative controls, so the developer can prove every step worked.

## For the owner: three problems, three fixes

In plain terms, ULTRON currently behaves like a person who waits for you to stop talking, then waits almost another second to be sure, then writes down everything you said before reading it, then re-reads their entire instruction binder from page one before thinking of an answer, then writes out a whole sentence before saying the first word of it. Each step is reasonable on its own. Done strictly one after another on a small CPU, they add up to a conversation that is "barely possible."

The first problem is the **brain's memory of the conversation**. ULTRON puts the current time (to the minute), what it overheard in the room, and whether you spoke or typed near the top of its instructions. Because of this, Ollama can't reuse any of the work it did on the previous turn. It also never tells Ollama how much conversation to hold, so Ollama falls back to a small default of 4,096 tokens and quietly drops older parts of long chats. Fixing this is mostly moving text around, and it is the single biggest speed-up available on a CPU.

The second problem is the **ears**. ULTRON has no real wake-word detector. It writes out *every* phrase anyone says near the microphone (TV included) to check whether it heard "Ultron." That keeps the computer busy, slows everything else down, and can even make ULTRON miss you while it is busy transcribing the TV. Purpose-built wake-word detectors cost a few percent of one processor core.

The third problem is the **voice**. ULTRON waits for a full sentence, creates all of its audio, sends it as one file, and only then starts playing. Speaking the first short phrase while generating the rest cuts the wait for the first word from about 3 seconds to about 1 second, with no change of voice.

Ask one question before spending money: does the PC have a graphics card, or a modern integrated graphics chip, that ULTRON isn't using? Every assistant in this survey that feels instant runs its brain on a GPU. If ULTRON's PC has one, turning it on matters more than anything else on this list. If it doesn't, the roadmap still makes ULTRON usable, and a small dedicated box or a phone can later act as its "handheld" front end.

## The 2025–2026 field splits into avatar apps and latency specialists

The local "Jarvis" landscape has four groups. The most-starred projects win on personality and visuals, not speed. **AIRI (about 51k stars)** is a TypeScript/Vue Electron companion with Live2D/VRM avatars, client-side speech detection, Kokoro TTS and Ollama support ([AIRI](https://github.com/moeru-ai/airi)). **Open-LLM-VTuber (about 14.5k)** is a Live2D assistant that runs fully offline on CPU or GPU, though its long-term memory is "temporarily removed" ([Open-LLM-VTuber](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Open-LLM-VTuber/Open-LLM-VTuber/main/README.md)). **Leon (about 17.6k)** is a TypeScript assistant whose NLU is "intents first … without relying on an LLM" ([Leon](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/leon-ai/leon/develop/README.md)). Next come frameworks and toolkits: Pipecat (about 17k), Hugging Face speech-to-speech (about 13.6k), and sherpa-onnx (about 15.7k), which matters most to ULTRON because it ships a Node addon covering streaming ASR, VAD, keyword spotting and TTS ([sherpa-onnx](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx)). Two patterns stand out. First, **Gemma 4 E2B/E4B has become the default small brain of 2026**: GLaDOS, Parlor, isair/jarvis and local-voice-ai all use it ([landscape](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/isair/jarvis/main/README.md)). So ULTRON's choice of gemma4:e2b matches the field. The difference is *how* the others run it: on llama.cpp with a GPU, with a warm and stable KV cache, fed partial transcripts or raw audio. Second, ULTRON's closest TypeScript peers (AIRI, Amica, Vocalis, IRIS-AI) publish no latency numbers. The latency playbook comes from Python projects, but every part of it (Silero VAD, Smart Turn, Parakeet, keyword spotting, Kokoro) is an ONNX model that Node can run through onnxruntime-node or `sherpa-onnx-node` ([sherpa-onnx](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx)). Note too that IRIS-AI's "<500 ms" relies on the cloud Gemini Live API, and ethanplusai/jarvis's brain is Claude Code. Neither is local, and their numbers are not comparable to ULTRON's ([IRIS](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/IRISX-AI/IRIS-AI/main/README.md)).

Evidence is uneven. Star counts are GitHub's rounded display values from 2026-11-01. Reddit, HN and YouTube content could be read mostly through mirrors and search snippets. **Discord content could not be searched at all**, so no Discord-sourced guidance appears here. Most latency claims are self-reported by builders. Vocalis's "<500 ms" has no stated hardware and should be treated as marketing.

## Fast assistants overlap five stages that ULTRON runs one after another

The synthesis across projects is consistent. Sub-second assistants **(1)** run a neural VAD in the client and end the turn after about 200–400 ms of silence, then ask a tiny semantic turn model whether the user is really finished. **(2)** They transcribe while the user speaks (streaming ASR), or feed audio straight into an audio-capable LLM. **(3)** They prefill the prompt cache, or even generate a speculative reply, during the pause, and throw it away if the user keeps going. **(4)** They keep a small quantized model resident on a GPU with thinking off and a **byte-stable prompt prefix**. **(5)** They stream LLM tokens into clause-sized TTS chunks with a 100–200 ms playback buffer, and cancel everything on barge-in. Smart Turn v3 makes step 1 cheap. It is an ~8M-parameter, 8 MB int8 ONNX model that takes "as little as 10ms on some CPUs" and about 65 ms on a cloud instance, and it is designed to run only when Silero VAD reports silence ([smart-turn](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pipecat-ai/smart-turn/main/README.md)). The YouTube build "How Fast Can a Fully Local Voice Agent Actually Get?" credits its main gains to moving from a sequential pipeline to overlapping transcription, generation, synthesis and playback ([summary](https://videohighlight.com/v/xbedfuqYQYA)).

**The prompt cache is the hidden giant on CPU.** On a CPU, time to first token is roughly uncached prompt tokens divided by prefill speed, and CPU prefill for 1–4B models runs only about 20–100 tokens/s ([itsfoss](https://itsfoss.com/testing-local-llms-without-gpu)). One builder measured 33 tok/s generation with Gemma 4 and still waited **8 s for the first token on a ~2,000-token prompt** ([Towards AI](https://pub.towardsai.net/my-local-gemma-4-ran-at-33-tokens-second-why-did-i-wait-eight-seconds-3fa027906220)). A CPU server took 55 s to first token on a 9,700-token prompt and **65 ms on the identical second call** thanks to the cache ([SSD Nodes](https://www.ssdnodes.com/learn/fix-ollama-context-deadline-exceeded)). Ollama reuses only the *matching prefix*. Anything that changes early in the prompt, such as a timestamp, recalled memories or a different tool list, forces everything after it to be recomputed ([Leanpub Ollama book](https://leanpub.com/read/ollama/prompt-caching)). Changing `num_ctx` between requests can also force a reload. Ollama's defaults make this worse: 4,096 tokens of context, models unloaded after 5 minutes, one parallel slot ([Ollama FAQ](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ollama/ollama/main/docs/faq.mdx)). Past `num_ctx`, Ollama **silently truncates the front of the prompt** ([jangwook.net](https://jangwook.net/en/tags/agentdesign/)).

**The serving stack matters less than its settings.** With equal settings, raw llama.cpp, LM Studio and Ollama are within about 0–13% on throughput ([InventiveHQ](https://inventivehq.com/blog/ollama-vs-llama-cpp-vs-lm-studio-benchmark)). One RTX 3060 test measured warm TTFT of about 180 ms for Ollama vs about 95 ms for llama-cpp-python ([dev.to](https://dev.to/jtorchia/the-local-llm-ecosystem-doesnt-need-ollama-and-that-made-me-uncomfortable-15fj)). That gap is real but small next to prefill. The reason to move to `llama-server` eventually is control: explicit slots, `cache_prompt`, slot save/restore ([llama.cpp #13606](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/13606)). Speculative decoding gave **1.01× on chat** on CPU and does not reduce prefill, so skip it ([InventiveHQ](https://inventivehq.com/blog/llama-cpp-speculative-decoding-consumer-gpu)). One caution for the future: llama.cpp issue #24714 documents forced full re-processing on SWA/hybrid-memory models (seen on Qwen3.5, not on gemma-4-E4B-it-qat in that test) ([#24714](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/24714)).

**Speech input has better options than Whisper base.en.** NVIDIA Parakeet TDT 0.6B v2 scores about 6% average WER on the Open ASR Leaderboard ([pinggy summary](https://pinggy.io/amp/blog/best_open_source_self_hosted_speech_to_text_models/)). It runs about **30× real time on an i7-12700KF** with ONNX ([groxaxo](https://github.com/groxaxo/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3-fastapi-openai)) and at RTF ~0.33 on a 2-vCPU box ([benchmark repost](https://bittide.aicompass.dev/article/cf69f0e5-d5d9-4cf3-a36d-fc6800a4bff0)). It is available in Node via sherpa-onnx. Moonshine drops Whisper's fixed 30-second padded window and "does work while the user is still talking." It has JS/WASM and Windows builds, and its vendor-reported latency is **59 ms (Medium Streaming) vs 11,286 ms for Whisper Large v3** from phrase end to text ([Moonshine](https://github.com/moonshine-ai/moonshine), [benchmarks](https://www.mintlify.com/moonshine-ai/moonshine/models/benchmarks)). For the wake word, sherpa-onnx's open-vocabulary keyword spotter (a ~3.3M-parameter Zipformer) takes "ULTRON" as tokens with no training ([sherpa KWS docs](https://k2-fsa.github.io/sherpa/onnx/kws/index.html)). openWakeWord runs 15–20 models on one Raspberry Pi 3 core and targets under 0.5 false accepts per hour ([openWakeWord](https://github.com/dscripka/openWakeWord)). For VAD, `@ricky0123/vad-web` ships Silero v5/v6 in the browser ([ricky0123/vad](https://github.com/ricky0123/vad)), and Parlor, Amica and voicechat2 use it.

**Speech output: the engine is mostly fine, the chunking is not.** kokoro-js already has true incremental streaming: `TextSplitterStream.push(token)` feeding `tts.stream()`, which yields audio chunks, with fp32/fp16/q8/q4 dtypes ([kokoro.js](https://github.com/hexgrad/kokoro/tree/main/kokoro.js)). Escalating chunk schedules are the standard pattern: a tiny first chunk, then sentences, then multi-sentence groups. KugelAudio uses minimum buffers of [5, 80, 150, 250] characters and one streaming session per turn, not per sentence ([KugelAudio](https://docs.kugelaudio.com/streaming-best-practices)). Kokoro-FastAPI warns that smaller chunks can add intonation artifacts ([Kokoro-FastAPI](https://github.com/remsky/Kokoro-FastAPI)). The strongest new CPU engine is **Kyutai Pocket TTS**: 100M parameters, about **200 ms to first chunk using 2 CPU cores**, streaming, voice cloning, MIT code, and a standalone Windows binary ([pocket-tts](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/pocket-tts)). Picovoice's harsher benchmark still put it about 2× faster than Kokoro on CPU (1,713 vs 3,658 ms) ([Picovoice](https://picovoice.ai/blog/on-device-tts/)). Full-duplex speech models (Moshi, PersonaPlex, MiniCPM-o 4.5) need 12–28 GB of VRAM and still struggle with tool calling ([MiniCPM-o 4.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27393v1), [arXiv 2609.19334](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19334)). Even Kyutai ships its own assistant as a cascade, so ULTRON should keep its cascade design.

## ULTRON spends its seconds in six serial waits

A read-only audit of ULTRON v0.26.0 (HEAD `d0fc9e3`; `npm test`: 277 passed, 21 skipped) traced a hands-free turn hop by hop. The repo's own measurements on 2-thread CI runners (`docs/PERFORMANCE.md`) put **hearing a phrase at 1.9–2.1 s, rising to 3.3–3.5 s while the voice is generating** (the processes share cores), and **first audio at 2.8–3.2 s** after the reply starts. A second voice process gave no reliable gain (0.95–1.20×). The table puts the hops together. The "today" column combines repo measurements and audit inferences. The target columns are estimates from the sources above and must be confirmed on the owner's PC.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| End of turn | 0.7 s fixed + 0–2.2 s "unfinished" hold | RMS VAD `END_FRAMES=35`; `HOLD_MS=2200` (`handsFree.ts:18,24`) | 0.7 s + ≤0.8 s, cancelled on speech | 0.25–0.4 s (Silero + Smart Turn) | same |

The worst offender is the prompt. The measured system prompt is **~7,850 characters (~2k tokens)** on a typical voice turn. The full tool schemas add 18.8k characters (all 42 tools, sent to cloud providers), or 0–1.4k when `focusTools` narrows them for Ollama. Content that changes each turn sits early. The overheard-room transcript is at about character 1,400. The voice-mode line, at offset 1,476, flips between typed and spoken turns. A tools-enabled/disabled sentence flips between small talk (0 focused tools) and tasks. `Current date/time` to the minute is at offset 2,761, followed by "you last talked N minutes ago" and the retrieved memories. Since the system message precedes up to 60k characters of history, **almost no turn reuses more than the first ~350 tokens of cache**, and prefill grows with every turn. That matches the owner's sense that conversations get slower the longer they go. No `options` object is sent at all: no `num_ctx`, `num_predict` or `num_thread`. That leaves the 4,096-token default against a history budget that can reach ~15k tokens, so long conversations are silently truncated and the prefix shifts further ([Ollama FAQ](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ollama/ollama/main/docs/faq.mdx)). `keep_alive` is inconsistent: 60m on warm-up, 30m on chat. The background Greeter and Summarizer call the same model with different system prompts, evicting the conversation's cache from Ollama's single slot. GLaDOS measured that with one slot a second concurrent request waited about 1.07 s, so it reserves slots for interactive work ([gemma4.md](https://github.com/dnhkng/GlaDOS/blob/main/docs/gemma4.md)). The good news: `think: false` is already sent, which avoids the Gemma 4 E2B "hidden thinking" trap that one benchmark found made it up to 20× slower ([thekodelab](https://thekodelab.com/en/posts/gemma4-e2b-vs-e4b-benchmark/)).

### Technical debt that will slow every future change

The structural debt clusters in five places. The first is **god-files**: `app.ts` has 974 lines with every route, and `chat.ts` has 866, with a 470-line `handleUserMessage` holding 8+ early-return intent branches. The second is **regex intent sprawl**: about 120 `.test(` calls across 33 files, including UI-side intents in `Chat.tsx:164,169` and a `save_memory` offer on any "my"/"I'm". The third is **synchronous work on the gateway's only event loop**: `DatabaseSync` SQLite, full-table loads just to count rows behind `/api/status` (every 5 s) and `/api/hud` (every 3 s), 1-second polls in settings views even though an SSE bus already exists, brute-force vector scans, MiniLM embeddings in-process, and an uncached recursive Start-menu walk on every "open X". The repo's own measurement shows in-process models causing multi-second event-loop freezes (3.7 s vs 38 ms). The fourth is **duplication**: the AudioWorklet and RMS code exist three times, `<think>` stripping four times, and the model lifecycle is near-identical in Whisper and Kokoro. The fifth is **missing tests and loose typing**: the latency-critical browser voice code (VAD, hold, barge-in, Speaker queue) has no unit tests, no test guards prompt-prefix stability or the `num_ctx` budget, there are 48 `any`s, and lint is just `tsc --noEmit`. Smaller fragilities: the production server and model children run TypeScript through `tsx` at runtime. A `JSON.parse` on each Ollama stream chunk and in the client SSE parser has no try/catch, so one malformed line kills the turn. `<think>` stripping breaks when a tag splits across chunks. The browser's ~6-connections-per-host limit can queue TTS fetches behind polls and two SSE streams. Some things are already right: one STT pass yields both the wake match and the command, TTS is already sentence-pipelined, and warm-up and `think: false` exist. These are foundations to build on, not replace.

## The roadmap runs from config fixes to streaming, then to hardware

Each phase can ship on its own and is ordered by latency gained per hour of work. "Impact" is the expected change in time from the end of the user's speech to the first audible word. These are estimates from the sources, and Phase 0 exists to replace them with measurements. Every test has a **positive control** (the fix produces the expected effect) and a **negative control** (deliberately undoing it or feeding a non-matching case reproduces the old behaviour or produces no effect). This proves the measurement can tell the two apart.

### Phase 0 — Measure before changing anything (1–2 days)

### Phase 4 — Optional bets to revisit, not build now

Gemma 4 E2B/E4B can take audio directly. GLaDOS measured E4B transcribing in 0.23–0.29 s vs Parakeet's 0.30–0.31 s warm ([gemma4.md](https://github.com/dnhkng/GlaDOS/blob/main/docs/gemma4.md)), and Parlor builds on this. But both run llama.cpp on a GPU, Parlor needs a June 2026 llama.cpp build, and Ollama's support for gemma4 audio input was not verified. Treat skipping STT as an experiment for after Phase 3, and only on a GPU. Full-duplex speech models (MiniCPM-o 4.5 needs ≥12 GB on llama.cpp-omni, and PersonaPlex is estimated at 14–16 GB) should stay on the watch list until they handle tools and persona reliably ([MiniCPM-o 4.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27393v1)). Packaging ULTRON as an Electron app (Node stays in-process; NSIS installer, auto-update, tray app with start at login) beats the PowerShell-launched browser window, and it avoids a Windows Service, which would run in session 0 without microphone access ([Tauri vs Electron](https://dolthub.com/blog/2025-11-13-electron-vs-tauri)). For the character voice, a Kokoro British male voice or voice mix plus a cheap DSP chain (−2 to −4 semitones, light ring modulation, a short metallic reverb), applied per chunk, gives an original "Ultron" sound. Cloning a film actor's voice carries likeness risk ([jarvis-voice recipe](https://claudemarket.ai/skills/globalcaos/jarvis-voice)).

## Handhelds work best as thin ears for a fast brain at home

Satellites and handhelds don't make the brain faster. They do wake word and echo cancellation locally, then stream audio to a server, so ULTRON's PC still sets the latency. The cheapest handheld is the **phone ULTRON already pairs over Tailscale**. Browsers expose the microphone only on HTTPS, and `tailscale serve` provides auto-renewed HTTPS for the `*.ts.net` name, which also makes the page installable as a PWA. Keep the app at the root path, because serving under a sub-path broke in one report ([Open WebUI HTTPS docs](https://docs.openwebui.com/reference/https.md), [Node-RED forum](https://discourse.nodered.org/t/when-is-it-advisable-to-use-https-with-tailscale/95251?page=2)). The fix is the same streaming design as Phase 3. The phone should stream Opus or PCM frames continuously with `echoCancellation` on, not upload 32 kB/s WAV clips per phrase, and should run the Silero VAD and wake word on the device.

For rooms around the house, the mature path is Home Assistant. The **Voice Preview Edition** (ESP32-S3 with an XMOS XU316 echo-cancelling audio front end) talks to HA Assist, and Nabu Casa recommends an Intel N100 or better for fully local processing ([Voice PE datasheet](https://voice-pe.home-assistant.io/resources/home_assistant_voice_preview_edition_datasheet_v1_1.pdf)). Exposing ULTRON as a conversation agent, or as an OpenAI-compatible endpoint that Assist calls, would let any HA satellite talk to ULTRON, and ULTRON already has an HA connector. Speaking Wyoming directly (one JSON header line plus PCM over TCP, with streaming transcript and synthesize events) is the second option ([Wyoming](https://github.com/OHF-Voice/wyoming)). Wyoming has no authentication, so keep it on the LAN or Tailscale. Avoid the $13 M5Stack Atom Echo: community threads report on-device wake word stopping after one detection and out-of-memory build failures ([HA community](https://community.home-assistant.io/t/m5stack-atom-echo-on-device-wake-word-with-microwakeword/752584?page=3)). The original wyoming-satellite was archived in January 2026 in favour of OHF-Voice/linux-voice-assistant for Pi-based satellites ([wyoming-satellite](https://github.com/sker65/wyoming-satellite)).

If the main PC has no GPU, the most interesting edge box is the **Jetson Orin Nano Super**. Community builds report whisper.cpp base.en at about 0.24 s and a Qwen2.5-3B short reply at about 0.44 s ([ancilla](https://github.com/Devcon324/ancilla)), and about 500 ms to first LLM token with Gemma E2B ([jetson-voice-assistant](https://maxmlang.github.io/jetson-voice-assistant/)). One Ollama + Kokoro build landed at 2.5–3.5 s total, a reminder that the software stack still matters ([Jarvis-home](https://github.com/itsMustafamr/Jarvis-home)). ULTRON could offload just the LLM to such a box via its OpenAI-compatible provider and keep everything else on the PC. NPUs are not ready to be the main brain. Lemonade's NPU path works only on Ryzen AI 300/400 under Windows ([Lemonade FAQ](https://lemonade-server.ai/faq)), and an Intel Lunar Lake NPU crashed on an 8B model and ran a 1B model at 2 tok/s ([elizaOS RFC](https://github.com/elizaOS/eliza/issues/7633)). They may suit always-on wake word or VAD later. Wearables such as Omi (an nRF5340 pendant with a published BLE audio protocol) would need a phone relay, and the official app has no confirmed local-LLM mode ([Omi BLE protocol](https://docs.omi.me/doc/developer/Protocol)).

## Conclusion

The research changes the diagnosis. ULTRON doesn't need a new architecture or a bigger computer to become conversational. It needs to stop throwing away work: the cache it rebuilds every turn, the CPU spent transcribing the TV, the speech it synthesizes after being interrupted, and the full sentence it waits for before speaking. The fastest projects of 2026 aren't winning on exotic models. They share ULTRON's model family and win by overlapping the stages and keeping caches warm. That is good news for a TypeScript codebase, because every needed part is available as ONNX that Node can run.

The honest limit is physics. Prefill on a few CPU cores caps a 2B model at a second or more for any new text, and no surveyed project has shown sub-second voice on CPU alone. So the order of work matters. Phase 0's hardware check may reveal an unused GPU that beats every software fix combined. If not, Phases 1–2 can make ULTRON usable on CPU, and a phone front end or a small Jetson box can later supply the "Jarvis" feel without replacing the PC. Because every step comes with a control test, the owner will see in measured numbers, not impressions, which changes earned their place.
